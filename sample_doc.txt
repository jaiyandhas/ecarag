Retrieval-Augmented Generation, commonly known as RAG, is a technique that
combines large language models with external document retrieval to produce
more accurate and grounded answers. Traditional RAG systems work in two
stages: first, a retriever finds the most relevant document chunks for a
given query using vector similarity search; second, a generator model uses
those chunks as context to produce a final answer.

A key weakness of traditional RAG is that the retriever's similarity search
can miss important information if the answer is spread across multiple
sections of a document, or if the most relevant passage is not the most
lexically similar one to the query. This is sometimes called semantic
drift, where retrieval quality directly caps generation quality regardless
of how good the underlying language model is.

Context-Aware RAG approaches address this by removing the initial retrieval
filter entirely. Instead of ranking chunks before generation, the document
is split into semantically coherent chunks, and every chunk is passed to
the language model to produce a candidate answer. After generation, each
candidate answer is scored against its source chunk using an embedding
similarity measure, such as cosine similarity between sentence embeddings.
Candidates that fall below a similarity threshold are discarded as
unsupported or hallucinated, and the highest-scoring remaining candidate is
returned as the final answer.

This project, ECA-RAG, extends that idea. After the initial bi-encoder
similarity filter discards clearly unsupported candidates, the remaining
candidates are re-scored using a cross-encoder model. Unlike a bi-encoder,
which embeds the answer and the chunk independently and compares the
resulting vectors, a cross-encoder processes the answer and chunk together
as a single input, allowing it to capture finer-grained alignment between
specific claims in the answer and specific statements in the chunk. This
two-stage validation is intended to catch cases where an answer shares
vocabulary with its source chunk, producing a deceptively high bi-encoder
similarity score, without actually being well-supported by the chunk's
content.

The system is evaluated by comparing the answer selected using bi-encoder
similarity alone against the answer selected after the additional
cross-encoder re-ranking stage, across a range of questions and documents,
to measure how often the two stages disagree and whether the cross-encoder
selection is more accurate when they do.
