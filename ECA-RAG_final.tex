\begin{abstract}
Dynamically sizing context windows in question answering seeks to supply adequate evidence while minimizing token waste. In this work, we rigorously benchmark calibrated decision thresholds and multi-hop gating rules on HotpotQA ($N=2640$ matched-depth). Although raw cross-encoder scores exhibit substantial miscalibration, Platt recalibration successfully lowers Expected Calibration Error from 0.225 to 0.039 and Brier score from 0.213 to 0.116 over 5,385 held-out evaluations ($N=540$). However, threshold-gated stopping fails to eclipse static context depths at the matched operating budget $k=4.00$: one-shot score gating trails fixed-$k$ by 2.7 points ($\Delta = -0.027$, Holm $p < 0.001$), while multi-step gated selection matches fixed-$k$ statistically ($\Delta = -0.0088$, raw $p=0.079$, Holm $p=0.079$), incurring a 2.6-point drop against unpruned iteration ($\Delta = -0.0257$, Holm $p < 0.001$). Furthermore, intermediate query expansion benefits bridge questions ($+1.7$ points, $\Delta = +0.017$, Holm $p=0.001$) but harms comparison queries ($-2.4$ points, $\Delta = -0.024$, Holm $p=0.007$). Exploratory sweeps indicate that a learned stopping classifier (ROC AUC 0.80) remains competitive with fixed-$k$ on bridge instances across $k \in [2.0, 3.5]$, but substantially underperforms on comparison problems ($\Delta = -0.138$ to $-0.044$, all Holm $p < 0.001$), while surpassing iterative score gating throughout (all Holm $p < 0.001$).
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Context selection, multi-hop inference, probability recalibration, dynamic retrieval horizon, cross-encoder scoring.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Augmenting generative language architectures with external textual databases provides a proven defense against non-factual generation while injecting domain-specific background~\cite{collini2025carag,asai2024selfrag}. Standard retrieval pipelines traditionally retrieve a predetermined, uniform passage count ($k$) regardless of query difficulty~\cite{kratzwald2018adaptive,jeong2024adaptiverag}. Such rigid allocation creates severe bottlenecks in multi-hop reasoning, which requires assembling scattered premises across non-contiguous documents~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}. When $k$ is small, critical bridging facts are missed; when $k$ is large, unhelpful text fragments crowd the prompt, introducing noise and increasing inference latency.

To address this trade-off, recent investigations propose dynamic stopping controllers that adjust retrieval volume per instance~\cite{kratzwald2018adaptive,jeong2024adaptiverag,wang2024targ,liu2024ctrla}. Common implementations filter passages by thresholding retriever confidence scores~\cite{collini2025carag}. However, modern deep neural classifiers—including dense cross-encoders—routinely exhibit miscalibration, assigning inflated confidence to erroneous predictions~\cite{guo2017calibration}. Because unnormalized model logits fail to reflect true posterior relevance probabilities, heuristic score cutoffs behave unpredictably across diverse reasoning types.

Here, we systematically evaluate how post-hoc probability scaling, sequential query expansion, and adaptive context truncation interact within multi-hop environments. Benchmarking six pipeline variants across 2,640 HotpotQA queries~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}, we uncover a recurring methodological pitfall: varying a stopping criterion alters both accuracy and the realized context volume ($k$). Consequently, naive comparisons can misattribute gains from larger context windows to smarter selection algorithms. To eliminate this confound, we enforce matched-budget benchmarking centered at $k = 4.00$, paired with exploratory non-parametric bootstrap evaluations ($B=10{,}000$) across an 8-point grid $k \in \{2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0, 4.5, 5.0, 5.5\}$.

Our principal empirical conclusions include:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Calibration Fidelity:} Uncalibrated cross-encoder logits show pronounced probability distortion on the held-out $N=540$ split ($\text{ECE}=0.2248$, Brier score $0.2127$, evaluated over the exact 5,385 candidate chunk predictions from the 540-example held-out split, not the $N=2640$ pool). Parametric Platt scaling~\cite{platt1999probabilistic} curtails ECE by 82.6\% to $0.0391$ and cuts Brier score to $0.1157$.
    \item \textbf{Calibrated Gating Boundaries at $k=4.00$:} Gating retrieval on calibrated chunk probabilities does not exceed static fixed-$k$ baselines at matched depth $k=4.00$. Calibrated single-pass gating underperforms fixed-$k$ re-ranking by $2.7$ points recall on bridge questions ($\Delta = -0.0266$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0341, -0.0191]$, Holm $p < 0.001$). Iterative gated retrieval yields recall statistically indistinguishable from fixed-$k$ on bridge questions ($\Delta = -0.0088$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0187, +0.0010]$, raw $p=0.0794$, Holm $p=0.0794$ within the 5-contrast bridge family at $k=4.00$, $95\%$ CI includes zero), but costs $2.6$ points recall relative to ungated iteration ($\Delta = -0.0257$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0328, -0.0186]$, Holm $p < 0.001$).
    \item \textbf{Structural Asymmetry in Query Expansion:} Hop-conditioned query reformulation at fixed depth $k=4.00$ yields a modest, statistically significant gain on bridge questions ($+1.7$ points, $\Delta = +0.017$, $95\%$ CI $[+0.0077, +0.0258]$, Holm $p=0.001$), but degrades comparison questions ($-2.4$ points, $\Delta = -0.024$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0391, -0.0089]$, Holm $p=0.007$), where concatenated text induces apparent query drift.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Depth Dynamics and Sufficiency Halting:} In exploratory multi-depth analysis, iterative gating lags behind fixed-$k$ at tight budgets ($k \le 3.5$, all Holm $p \le 0.005$) and converges with fixed-$k$ from $k=4.0$ to $k=5.0$. At $k=5.5$, it attains an exploratory $+0.0127$ recall ($95\%$ CI $[+0.0044, +0.0210]$, Holm $p=0.0176$). A learned sufficiency stopping classifier (ROC AUC $0.7964$) remains statistically indistinguishable from fixed-$k$ on bridge questions across all covered depths ($k \in [2.0, 3.5]$, with 95\% CIs spanning zero). However, on comparison questions, the stopper is significantly worse than fixed-$k$ at every covered depth $k \in [2.0, 3.5]$ ($\Delta = -0.138$ to $-0.044$, all Holm $p < 0.001$). Nonetheless, in exploratory analysis it improves upon iterative gating (Arm 5) on both question types at $k \in [2.0, 3.5]$ ($+1.5$ to $+5.3$ points on bridge, $+3.4$ to $+5.5$ points on comparison, all Holm $p < 0.001$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Context Validation and Re-Ranking}
Standard dense search employs dual-encoder networks that map queries and texts into embedding spaces independently. While enabling rapid approximate nearest-neighbor lookups, dual encoders lack token-level cross-interaction. Second-stage cross-encoders address this limitation by executing full cross-attention over concatenated query--passage pairs, substantially boosting precision. Context-Aware RAG (CA-RAG)~\cite{collini2025carag} introduced semantic consistency filtering to suppress irrelevant context and prevent generation anomalies. In our experimental setup, Arm 1 replicates this CA-RAG dual-encoder baseline, against which we systematically benchmark full cross-attention scoring, probability recalibration, and dynamic multi-step stopping.

\subsection{Dynamic Retrieval Horizontally and Vertically}
Dynamic RAG frameworks adjust lookup behavior based on query traits. Prior studies investigate varying retrieval scope per query: Kratzwald and Feuerriegel~\cite{kratzwald2018adaptive} explored adaptive search depths for open-domain QA, while Adaptive-RAG~\cite{jeong2024adaptiverag} routes queries into zero-, single-, or multi-step execution paths via a dedicated complexity predictor. Training-free alternatives like TARG~\cite{wang2024targ} (leveraging speculative draft entropy) and CtrlA~\cite{liu2024ctrla} (probing model internal activation directions) determine whether external context is required. During long-form generation, active systems such as FLARE~\cite{jiang2023flare}, Self-RAG~\cite{asai2024selfrag}, and DRAGIN~\cite{su2024dragin} trigger retrieval whenever token-level uncertainty spikes. However, existing work predominantly targets the binary choice of whether to retrieve, leaving the question of optimal passage budget ($k$) under strictly controlled depth budgets underexplored.

\subsection{Confidence Calibration in Pretrained Models}
Deep neural networks notoriously produce uncalibrated confidence estimates that deviate from actual accuracy~\cite{guo2017calibration}. Post-hoc recalibration schemes, including Platt scaling~\cite{platt1999probabilistic} and temperature scaling, parameterize monotonic transformations on calibration subsets to align raw logits with empirical correctness. In this study, we ask whether such recalibrated scores provide effective, trustworthy termination thresholds for multi-hop retrieval.

\subsection{Multi-Hop Retrieval Pipelines}
Resolving multi-step questions requires following interconnected entity paths spanning separate text blocks. Dedicated architectures like Multi-hop Dense Retrieval (MDR)~\cite{xiong2021mdr} and Baleen~\cite{khattab2021baleen} train specialized multi-step encoders to traverse document topologies. Similarly, IRCoT~\cite{trivedi2023ircot} interleaves chain-of-thought prompting with iterative passage collection. In contrast to training bespoke multi-hop encoders, our study tests whether post-hoc confidence calibration and iterative halting can regulate retrieval depth using standard, off-the-shelf retrieval models.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{System Architecture and Evaluation Arms}
We benchmark six distinct retrieval methodologies (Arms 1--6) operating over the standard candidate pool of $\sim 10$ passages per HotpotQA item in the distractor configuration:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Arm 1 (Dense Baseline):} Encodes query $q$ and retrieves the top-$k$ passages ranked by cosine similarity using a bi-encoder: $\mathcal{D}_1 = \text{Top-}k_{d \in \mathcal{P}} \langle \mathbf{e}_q, \mathbf{e}_d \rangle$, where $\mathcal{P}$ is the per-question candidate pool.
    \item \textbf{Arm 2 (Fixed-$k$ Cross-Encoder):} Retrieves an initial candidate pool of $M=10$ passages using Arm 1, then computes full cross-attention relevance logits $s(q, d)$ with a cross-encoder (truncated to 512 tokens). Passages are selected under fixed integer cutoffs $k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$.
    \item \textbf{Arm 3 (Uncalibrated Marginal Gating):} Traverses the ranked passage list generated by the cross-encoder, retaining candidates whose unnormalized logit satisfies $s(q, d) \ge s_{\text{thresh}}$, bounded between a minimum of 1 and a maximum of 6 chunks.
    \item \textbf{Arm 4 (Calibrated Single-Pass Gating):} Maps raw logits $s$ into calibrated probabilities $\hat{p} = \sigma(a \cdot s + b)$ via Platt scaling and steps through the ordered list, selecting items meeting $\hat{p} \ge \theta$, constrained between 1 and 6 chunks. We examine both global pooled scaling (\textbf{Arm 4a}) and question-type-conditioned parameters (\textbf{Arm 4b}).
    \item \textbf{Arm 5 (Calibrated Iterative Adaptive-$k$):} Performs iterative multi-hop traversal up to 6 hops over the per-question pool. At hop 0, remaining passages are evaluated against query $q$ alone; at each successive hop $h \ge 1$, the cross-encoder re-evaluates all remaining candidates against $q$ concatenated with the full text of the most recently retrieved chunk ($q \oplus \text{ `` ''} \oplus d_{(h)}$). The top candidate is chosen; for hops $h \ge 1$, traversal terminates whenever its per-type Platt probability falls below $\theta$ (max 6 chunks). No bi-encoder is re-invoked once the initial candidate set is formed; a 50-word prefix truncation baseline is analyzed strictly as an ablation in Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}.
    \item \textbf{Arm 6 (Learned Sufficiency Stopping):} Implements a supervised logistic regression classifier with standardized feature preprocessing trained on calibration traces. The model leverages 6 features: calibrated probability of the incoming candidate, its raw logit, hop index, probability differential from the preceding hop, minimum calibrated probability among already selected passages, and a binary indicator for bridge questions (\texttt{is\_bridge}). The binary target signals whether all gold supporting passages have been accumulated. At each hop $h \ge 1$, retrieval halts if candidate confidence drops below $\theta$ or if the posterior $P(\text{sufficient} \mid \mathbf{x}) \ge \tau$ (subject to a 6-chunk maximum).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Chunk-Level Platt Calibration}
Given a cross-encoder assigning raw continuous scores $(q, d) \to s \in \mathbb{R}$, we fit an affine logit adjustment passed through the standard logistic sigmoid function~\cite{platt1999probabilistic}:
\begin{equation}
    P(y = 1 \mid s) = \sigma(a \cdot s + b) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-(a \cdot s + b))}
\end{equation}
where $y \in \{0, 1\}$ represents ground-truth supporting document relevance. Parameters $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ are determined on the calibration set by minimizing binary cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(a, b) = -\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{cal}}} \left[ y_i \log \hat{p}_i + (1 - y_i) \log(1 - \hat{p}_i) \right]
\end{equation}
Calibration fidelity is measured via Expected Calibration Error (ECE) across $M=10$ uniform probability bins $B_m \subset (0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{|B_m|}{N} \left| \text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m) \right|
\end{equation}
paired with the mean squared probability error (Brier score): $\text{BS} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2$.

\subsection{Hop-Conditioned Iterative Re-Ranking}
In multi-hop settings, early search steps often retrieve an introductory passage whose textual body uncovers bridging entities necessary for subsequent inference. In Arm 5 and ungated multi-step retrieval, traversal iterates up to 6 hops over the per-query pool of $\sim 10$ candidates without re-executing bi-encoder retrieval. At hop 0, candidate texts are matched against $q$ alone. At each subsequent hop $h \ge 1$, the cross-encoder re-ranks all unselected candidates against the prompt augmented with the complete passage selected at hop $h-1$:
\begin{equation}
    q^{(h+1)} = q \oplus \text{ `` ''} \oplus d_{(h)}
\end{equation}
The highest-ranking available candidate is extracted. In Arm 5, for hops $h \ge 1$, search halts if the candidate's type-specific Platt probability drops below $\theta$ (min 1, max 6 chunks). In Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}, we contrast this complete passage concatenation with a 50-word prefix truncation variant ($q \oplus \text{ `` ''} \oplus \text{Trunc}_{50}(d_{(h)})$) to verify whether excessive passage length induces token dilution during cross-attention re-ranking.

\subsection{Reproducibility Protocol and Frozen Parameters}
To ensure reproducibility:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dataset and Splits:} We use the HotpotQA validation set in the distractor setting ($N=3000$ total examples drawn from the full validation set of 7,405 examples, each paired with a candidate pool of $\sim 10$ paragraphs)~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}. The 3,000 examples are the first 3,000 of the seed-42 shuffled HotpotQA distractor validation set. A seed-42 random split partitioned the first 900 example IDs into the first 40\% ($N=360$; 279 bridge, 81 comparison) for calibration and the remaining 60\% ($N=540$; 424 bridge, 116 comparison) for held-out evaluation (these are not contiguous index slices of the unshuffled dataset). The extension split ($N=2100$; 1653 bridge, 447 comparison) comprises elements 900 to 2999 from the same seed-42 shuffled HotpotQA distractor validation set; its example IDs are verified to be strictly disjoint from the first 900 examples. The pooled dataset ($N=2640$; 2077 bridge, 563 comparison) concatenates the evaluation and extension splits.
    \item \textbf{Models and Versions:} Dense retrieval uses \texttt{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2} (384-dimensional embeddings, cosine similarity). Cross-encoder re-ranking uses \texttt{cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2} with a 512-token truncation limit.
    \item \textbf{Frozen Calibration Parameters:} Parameters fit on the $N=360$ calibration split are held frozen across all evaluation splits:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Platt parameters: Pooled ($a=0.2703, b=-1.3639$); Bridge ($a=0.2454, b=-1.3621$); Comparison ($a=0.4724, b=-1.4665$).
        \item Confidence threshold: $\theta^* = 0.18$ was selected on the calibration split to maximize recall subject to mean depth $k \le 4.0$. Sensitivity is evaluated at $\theta = 0.15$.
        \item Uncalibrated threshold: $s_{\text{thresh}} = -1.9250$ (median logit on calibration split).
        \item Sufficiency stopper: $\tau^* = 0.95$ (tuned on calibration traces).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Seed:} All data partitioning and bootstrap iterations use seed 42.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Calibration Quality}
Table~\ref{tab:calibration} reports calibration metrics for raw versus Platt-scaled cross-encoder logits on the held-out $N=540$ evaluation set (exact 5,385 candidate chunk predictions, not the pooled $N=2640$ cohort). Raw logits mapped through a standard sigmoid display high miscalibration ($\text{ECE}=0.2248$, Brier score $0.2127$), overestimating the probability of relevance for negative distractor paragraphs.

Platt scaling reduces ECE to $0.0419$ (pooled) and $0.0391$ (per-type), achieving an $82.6\%$ relative reduction in calibration error. The Brier score drops by $45.6\%$ to $0.1157$. Conditioning calibration on question type provides a minor gain, reflecting slightly different logit distributions between bridge and comparison queries. Platt parameters transferred robustly to the $N=2100$ extension split (20,901 candidate chunk predictions): raw logits exhibited $\text{ECE}=0.2143$ and Brier score $0.2039$, whereas frozen per-type Platt scaling achieved $\text{ECE}=0.0373$ (an $82.6\%$ relative reduction) and Brier score $0.1146$ (pooled Platt: $\text{ECE}=0.0389$, Brier score $0.1162$), demonstrating that the calibration parameters generalize stably across independent question sets.

\subsection{Matched-Depth Evaluation at Reference Depth $k=4.00$}
Table~\ref{tab:matched_depth} presents our matched-depth results at exact mean depth $k=4.00$, comparing static fixed-$k$ retrieval against ungated iterative retrieval and calibrated adaptive gating across the pooled $N=2640$ cohort as well as the constituent $N=540$ and $N=2100$ splits.

\subsubsection{Bridge Questions}
On the pooled bridge cohort ($N=2077$), ungated iterative retrieval attains a recall of $0.8240$, compared to $0.8072$ for fixed-$k$ retrieval ($\Delta = +0.0169$, $95\%$ CI $[+0.0077, +0.0258]$, $p < 0.001$, Holm $p=0.001$). Gating retrieval on calibrated scores does not improve recall over fixed-$k$: Arm 5 attains an interpolated recall of $0.7983$, displaying no statistically significant divergence from fixed-$k$ ($\Delta = -0.0088$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0187, +0.0010]$, raw $p = 0.0794$, Holm $p = 0.0794$).

Relative to ungated iteration at identical depth, gating incurs a recall cost of $2.6$ points ($\Delta = -0.0257$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0328, -0.0186]$, $p < 0.001$, Holm $p < 0.001$). Calibrated single-pass gating (Arm 4b) performs significantly below fixed-$k$ at matched depth, reaching $0.7806$ recall ($\Delta = -0.0266$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0341, -0.0191]$, $p < 0.001$).

\subsubsection{Comparison Questions}
On the pooled comparison cohort ($N=563$), static fixed-$k$ retrieval attains $0.9538$ recall. Ungated iterative search attains a lower recall of $0.9298$ ($\Delta = -0.0240$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0391, -0.0089]$, $p = 0.002$, Holm $p=0.007$). Arm 4b calibrated single-pass gating achieves $0.9520$ recall, which is indistinguishable from fixed-$k$ ($\Delta = -0.0018$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0136, +0.0095]$, raw $p = 0.7202$, Holm $p = 0.7202$). Iterative gated retrieval (Arm 5) reaches $0.9147$ ($\Delta = -0.0391$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0565, -0.0221]$, $p < 0.001$).

Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto} shows the empirical Pareto frontiers of Paragraph Recall versus Mean Retrieval Depth ($k$) across bridge and comparison subsets on the pooled $N=2640$ dataset, displaying pointwise 95\% bootstrap confidence bands ($B=10{,}000$).

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ablation: Full-Chunk vs. 50-Word Query Augmentation in Arm 5}
\label{sec:results-ablation}
To evaluate whether appending the full previously selected chunk introduces query drift or token dilution during later hops of cross-encoder re-ranking, we compare full-chunk augmentation (default Arm 5) against a 50-word prefix truncation variant on the $N=540$ evaluation split across both operating thresholds ($\theta^*=0.18$ and sensitivity $\theta=0.15$). We note that Arm 5 is iterative and hop-conditioned, concatenating previously selected chunks into queries for later hops, whereas Arm 4b is single-pass and has no query augmentation. This ablation is a single-split descriptive comparison with no significance test:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{At Operating Threshold $\theta^*=0.18$:} Full-chunk augmentation achieves bridge recall $0.7983$ ($k=3.934$, precision $0.5107$, full-match $0.6108$) and comparison recall $0.8793$ ($k=4.198$, precision $0.4947$, full-match $0.7759$). Truncating to a 50-word prefix yields bridge recall $0.8031$ ($k=4.033$, precision $0.4983$, full-match $0.6179$) and comparison recall $0.9009$ ($k=4.328$, precision $0.4864$, full-match $0.8190$).
    \item \textit{At Sensitivity Threshold $\theta=0.15$:} Full-chunk augmentation attains bridge recall $0.8243$ ($k=4.455$, precision $0.4523$, full-match $0.6604$) and comparison recall $0.9138$ ($k=4.560$, precision $0.4701$, full-match $0.8362$). Truncating to 50 words yields identical bridge recall of $0.8243$ ($k=4.493$, precision $0.4494$, full-match $0.6580$) and comparison recall $0.9353$ ($k=4.595$, precision $0.4691$, full-match $0.8793$).
\end{itemize}
The observed differences between 50-word prefix truncation and full-chunk augmentation in Arm 5 are: bridge recall is $+0.005$ at $\theta^*=0.18$ and $0.000$ at $\theta=0.15$; comparison recall is $+0.022$ at $\theta^*=0.18$ and $+0.022$ at $\theta=0.15$, with $k$ also higher by $\sim 0.1$ chunks in each case.

% V. DISCUSSION & MECHANISTIC HYPOTHESES
% =========================================================================
\enlargethispage{4\baselineskip}
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Our empirical findings reveal that neither post-hoc calibration nor confidence-based gating enables adaptive-$k$ retrieval to consistently surpass static fixed-depth baselines in multi-hop RAG. We offer three mechanistic hypotheses to interpret these observations.

\subsection{Hypothesis 1: Chunk-Level Calibration vs. Set-Level Sufficiency}
We hypothesize that the core bottleneck of calibrated gating stems from the tension between \textit{chunk-level relevance calibration} and \textit{set-level evidence sufficiency}. Platt scaling is designed to ensure that among candidate chunks assigned predicted probability $\hat{p} \approx 0.30$, approximately 30\% constitute genuine supporting documents in distribution—an alignment verified empirically, reducing ECE to $0.0391$ on evaluation and $0.0373$ on extension. Yet resolving multi-hop questions requires assembling disjoint supporting facts jointly. An independent stopping rule that drops candidates when individual probability falls below $\theta$ prematurely severs multi-hop chains: bridge passages often share less initial lexical overlap with the query than first-hop texts. Consequently, chunk-level thresholding prunes reasoning chains mid-traversal, degrading set-level recall at tight budgets.

\subsection{Hypothesis 2: Asymmetric Query Reformulation Dynamics}
The polarized impact of hop-conditioned query augmentation—advancing bridge questions ($+1.7$ points, $\Delta = +0.017$) while impairing comparison queries ($-2.4$ points, $\Delta = -0.024$)—stems directly from the underlying graph topology of the reasoning task:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Bridge Questions:} Bridge queries follow a sequential chain ($A \to B \to C$). The prompt mentions entity $A$, while evidence regarding $C$ requires uncovering intermediate link entity $B$. Concatenating the text of the previously retrieved chunk injects entity $B$ into the cross-encoder input, bridging the semantic gap.
    \item \textbf{Comparison Questions (Hypothesized Query Drift):} Comparison queries follow a parallel structure ($A \text{ vs. } B$), explicitly mentioning both target entities in the initial question (e.g., \textit{``Were Scott Derrickson and Ed Wood of the same nationality?''}). We hypothesize that appending context describing entity $A$ over-indexes cross-encoder attention onto $A$, crowding out entity $B$ during subsequent re-ranking steps. This hypothesized query drift impairs comparison recall.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hypothesis 3: Oracle Routing as an Upper Bound}
Our assessment of the Oracle Hybrid pipeline demonstrates that optimal retrieval policy is intrinsically tied to question topology. While score-level thresholding fails to reliably beat fixed-$k$, routing queries according to reasoning topology achieves $0.8240$ bridge recall and $0.9538$ comparison recall at $k=4.00$. However, because this upper bound presumes access to ground-truth question classifications, practical deployments must prioritize topology-detection classifiers prior to context assembly.

\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}
\label{sec:threats}
We outline eight primary limitations governing our study:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Benchmark Generalization:} Our investigation is grounded within HotpotQA~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}. Although HotpotQA represents a canonical multi-hop benchmark, alternate datasets (such as 2WikiMultiHopQA and MuSiQue) present differing reasoning hop depths, negative distractor distributions, and entity graph topologies.
    \item \textbf{Distractor Setting Scope:} All experiments operate in HotpotQA's distractor setting, evaluating a per-query pool of $\sim 10$ passages (2 ground-truth facts, 8 challenging distractors). While this protocol isolates re-ranking precision and threshold behavior against hard negatives, it does not assess full-scale open-domain retrieval across web-scale corpora.
    \item \textbf{Focus on Retrieval Metrics:} Our evaluations focus on retrieval-stage fidelity—specifically passage recall, precision, and full-match rate. While retrieval recall bounds context availability for generation, we do not assess downstream language model synthesis metrics (e.g., Exact Match or token F1).
    \item \textbf{Model Architectures:} We investigated a single bi-encoder (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) and a single cross-encoder (\texttt{ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2}). Larger model scales (e.g., 7B-parameter cross-encoders or generative re-rankers) may display distinct calibration characteristics and representational power.
    \item \textbf{Context Window Truncation:} Cross-encoder inputs were capped at 512 tokens, which may occasionally truncate passage content during concatenated multi-hop re-ranking.
    \item \textbf{Reliance on Oracle Question Types:} The hybrid upper bound and Arm 6's \texttt{is\_bridge} feature depend on ground-truth question-type metadata. Real-world systems must predict question topology dynamically.
    \item \textbf{Hyperparameter Selection Split:} Decision cutoffs ($\theta^*=0.18$, $\tau^*=0.95$, $s_{\text{thresh}}=-1.9250$) were established using the $N=360$ calibration subset. Alternate data distributions may favor different decision thresholds.
    \item \textbf{Exploratory Grid Selection and Arm 6 Coverage:} The multi-depth grid was chosen post-hoc after inspecting empirical Pareto curves, and depth $k=5.5$ sits at the parameter sweep margin where bootstrap coverage is constrained; thus, the gain ($+0.0127$, Holm $p=0.0176$) is exploratory. Additionally, on bridge questions Arm 6 plateaus at mean depth $k=3.87$ and never reaches $k=4.00$; on comparison questions, point estimates reach $k \approx 4.04\text{--}4.20$, but fewer than 95\% of resamples cover $k=4.00$, precluding non-parametric hypothesis testing.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reproducibility}
Experimental pipelines used Python 3.14, PyTorch 2.12.1, Hugging Face \texttt{transformers} 5.12.1, \texttt{sentence-transformers} 6.1.0, \texttt{scikit-learn} 1.8.0, and \texttt{datasets} 5.0.0. From the 7,405 validation items in HotpotQA's distractor setting, we applied seed-42 shuffling and partitioned the initial 3,000 instances into calibration ($N=360$), evaluation ($N=540$), and extension ($N=2100$) splits. All calibration parameters (Platt coefficients, $\theta^*=0.18$, $\tau^*=0.95$, $s_{\text{thresh}}=-1.9250$) were fit once on the calibration split and held frozen across all evaluation experiments.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This study evaluated post-hoc confidence calibration, hop-conditioned query reformulation, and adaptive retrieval depth in multi-hop RAG. Although Platt scaling reduces cross-encoder calibration error by over 80\%, gating retrieval decisions on calibrated probabilities fails to outperform static fixed-$k$ baselines at matched depth $k=4.00$. Furthermore, confidence-based gating imposes a noticeable recall penalty relative to ungated multi-step iteration on bridge questions. In exploratory multi-depth analysis across $k \in [2.0, 5.5]$, iterative gating lags behind fixed-$k$ at constrained budgets before matching fixed-$k$ at intermediate depths; on bridge questions, a learned sufficiency stopper matches fixed-$k$ across all covered depths (all 95\% CIs include zero), but on comparison questions it is significantly worse than fixed-$k$ at every covered depth $k \in [2.0, 3.5]$ ($\Delta = -0.138$ to $-0.044$, all Holm $p < 0.001$); nonetheless, in exploratory evaluation it improves upon iterative gating (Arm 5) on both question types across $k \in [2.0, 3.5]$ by $+1.5$ to $+5.5$ points (all Holm $p < 0.001$). Sequential query expansion yields clear benefits for chained bridge questions while impairing parallel comparison queries. These findings show that adaptive retrieval depth cannot be resolved through passage-level score thresholding alone; future research in multi-hop RAG should focus on topology-guided query routing and set-level evidence sufficiency.
